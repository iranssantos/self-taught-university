# Bachelor of Science in Mathematics — Program Handbook

Self-Taught University · Version 1.0 · October 2026

## Program Overview

The Bachelor of Science in Mathematics is a self-directed undergraduate program of 32 courses and 5,440 nominal hours, modeled on the structure of MIT Course 18 (Mathematics). It is designed for adults who want a complete mathematics education studied at their own pace, with the record of their work open for anyone to examine.

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Format | Self-directed study with published textbooks and open reference courses |
| Courses required | 32: 17 fixed, 11 electives, and 4 free electives, with 2 of the electives taken as seminars |
| Nominal hours | 170 per course (equivalent to a 12-unit MIT subject); 5,440 in total |
| Reference pace | 2 courses per 4-month term, about 20 hours of study per week |
| Reference length | 16 terms, about 5 years and 4 months |
| Language | English |
| Cost | None for the program; textbooks as chosen by the student |

The program is inspired by MIT Course 18 and uses MIT OpenCourseWare and other university materials as reference courses. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to MIT or any other institution. Completing it does not confer an accredited degree; the credential rests on the work the student publishes (see Credential and Verification).

## Mission and Program Learning Outcomes

The program's mission is to give any motivated adult the mathematical education of a strong undergraduate major, and to replace institutional endorsement with openly verifiable work.

Graduates of the program are able to:

1. Read, write, and critique rigorous proofs in analysis, algebra, topology, and discrete mathematics.
2. Use the core tools of single and multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and differential equations to model and solve problems.
3. Reason about uncertainty with probability and statistics, from definitions to inference.
4. Work with the central structures of modern mathematics: groups, rings, fields, vector spaces, metric and topological spaces.
5. Select and justify methods in at least one area of depth chosen from the electives.
6. Apply mathematics to physical systems and to computation.
7. Communicate mathematics in writing and in an oral presentation to a mathematically literate audience.
8. Study independently from primary texts, plan long-term work, and document it so that others can verify it.

## Degree Requirements

The program follows the blocks of an MIT bachelor's degree. At MIT, every student takes 17 institute requirements, the subjects of the major, and free electives. Here the 17 fixed courses keep the subjects every MIT mathematics major takes and replace the institute requirements outside the field (chemistry, biology, humanities) with mathematics and computing, so the whole degree stays in the field.

| Requirement | Courses | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed courses | 17 | The same for every student. Nine are taken in some form by every MIT mathematics major: single and multivariable calculus, physics I and II, differential equations, linear algebra, real analysis, probability and statistics, and abstract algebra I. Two more are in mathematics: complex variables and topology. Six are in computing with mathematical content: mathematics for computer science, programming in Python, introduction to algorithms, theory of computation, cryptography, and mathematical logic |
| Electives | 11 | Chosen by the student from the mathematics electives and topics courses |
| Free electives | 4 | Chosen from the whole catalog: any course can be counted as a free elective |
| Seminars | 2 | Topics courses taken as a seminar, assessed by a recorded presentation and a paper; they still count as electives |
| **Total** | **32** | **5,440 nominal hours** |

- **Classification.** Courses follow MIT's classification of their reference subjects: subjects of MIT's mathematics department are electives, subjects of other departments are free electives, and communication-intensive subjects can be taken as seminars. Any course can be counted as a free elective, so no course is wasted.
- **Course weight.** Every course carries 170 nominal hours, the workload of a 12-unit MIT subject (about 12 hours per week for 14 weeks).
- **Prerequisites.** Each course lists its prerequisites. Taking a course before its prerequisites is allowed but flagged; the student is responsible for any gap.
- **Course numbers.** 100-level courses are introductory, 200-level intermediate, 300-level advanced, and 400-level topics courses and seminars. MATH, CS, and PHYS mark the field.

## Program Structure

The reference plan takes 2 courses per 4-month term, 3 terms per year. In the first year (terms 1–3) both courses are fixed. From the second year on, as at MIT, each term has one fixed course and one course the student chooses, so electives and seminars start early and run alongside the fixed courses. Terms are flexible: each term starts when the previous one ends, and finishing early or late moves every later term with it.

## Course Catalog: Fixed Courses

Every course carries 170 hours. Texts list the primary text first, with chapters where they apply; check chapters against your edition.

### MATH 101 Single Variable Calculus

Prerequisites: none. Limits and continuity; derivatives and their applications to rates, approximation, and optimization; the definite integral and the fundamental theorem of calculus; techniques and applications of integration; sequences and series, including power and Taylor series.

Texts: Stewart, Clegg, and Watson, *Calculus: Early Transcendentals*, 9th ed., ch. 1–11; Hass, Heil, and Weir, *Thomas' Calculus*, 14th ed. Reference: [MIT 18.01](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-01sc-single-variable-calculus-fall-2010/).

### MATH 110 Mathematics for Computer Science

Prerequisites: none. Proofs by induction, contradiction, and construction; sets, relations, functions, and logic; number theory and modular arithmetic; graphs and trees; counting, generating functions, and recurrences; discrete probability.

Texts: Lehman, Leighton, and Meyer, *Mathematics for Computer Science*, Parts I–III; Velleman, *How to Prove It*, 3rd ed., ch. 1–6. Reference: [MIT 18.062J / 6.042J](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-042j-mathematics-for-computer-science-spring-2015/).

### CS 101 Introduction to Programming in Python

Prerequisites: none. The introduction to computer science and algorithms through programming: variables, control flow, and functions; strings, lists, and dictionaries; recursion; object-oriented programming; testing and debugging; searching and sorting, and an introduction to algorithmic complexity.

Texts: Guttag, *Introduction to Computation and Programming Using Python*, 3rd ed.; Downey, *Think Python*, 2nd ed. Reference: [MIT 6.0001](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-0001-introduction-to-computer-science-and-programming-in-python-fall-2016/).

### PHYS 101 Classical Mechanics

Prerequisites: MATH 101. Kinematics; Newton's laws; work and energy; momentum and collisions; rotational motion and angular momentum; gravitation; oscillations.

Texts: Halliday, Resnick, and Walker, *Fundamentals of Physics*, 10th ed., ch. 1–13, 15; Kleppner and Kolenkow, *An Introduction to Mechanics*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–7. Reference: [MIT 8.01SC](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/).

### MATH 102 Multivariable Calculus

Prerequisites: MATH 101. Vectors and the geometry of space; partial derivatives, gradients, and optimization with constraints; double and triple integrals; vector fields, line and surface integrals; the theorems of Green, Stokes, and Gauss.

Texts: Stewart et al., ch. 12–16; Hass et al. Reference: [MIT 18.02](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-02sc-multivariable-calculus-fall-2010/).

### MATH 120 Linear Algebra

Prerequisites: MATH 101. Systems of linear equations and elimination; matrix algebra; the four fundamental subspaces; orthogonality, projections, and least squares; determinants; eigenvalues and diagonalization; symmetric and positive definite matrices; the singular value decomposition.

Texts: Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*, 6th ed., ch. 1–7; Lang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*, 2nd ed. Reference: [MIT 18.06](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/).

### PHYS 102 Electricity and Magnetism

Prerequisites: PHYS 101, MATH 102. Electric fields and Gauss's law; electric potential; capacitance and circuits; magnetic fields; induction; Maxwell's equations and electromagnetic waves.

Texts: Halliday, Resnick, and Walker, ch. 21–33; Purcell and Morin, *Electricity and Magnetism*, 3rd ed., ch. 1–9. Reference: [MIT 8.02](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/).

### MATH 130 Differential Equations

Prerequisites: MATH 101. First-order equations and their models; linear equations of higher order; the Laplace transform; systems of linear equations and phase portraits; nonlinear systems and stability; series solutions and an introduction to Fourier series.

Texts: Boyce, DiPrima, and Meade, *Elementary Differential Equations and Boundary Value Problems*, 11th ed., ch. 1–7, 9; Simmons, *Differential Equations with Applications and Historical Notes*, 3rd ed. Reference: [MIT 18.03](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/).

### CS 120 Introduction to Algorithms

Prerequisites: MATH 110, CS 101. Asymptotic analysis; sorting; data structures, including heaps, hash tables, and balanced search trees; graph search; shortest paths; dynamic programming.

Texts: Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, and Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, 4th ed., ch. 1–4, 6–8, 10–14, 20, 22; Erickson, *Algorithms*. Reference: [MIT 6.006](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-006-introduction-to-algorithms-spring-2020/).

### MATH 140 Probability and Statistics

Prerequisites: MATH 102. Probability spaces, conditional probability, and Bayes' theorem; discrete and continuous random variables; expectation, variance, and the law of large numbers; the central limit theorem; estimation, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing; Bayesian updating.

Texts: DeGroot and Schervish, *Probability and Statistics*, 4th ed., ch. 1–9; Ross, *A First Course in Probability*, 10th ed. Reference: [MIT 18.05](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2014/).

### MATH 210 Real Analysis

Prerequisites: MATH 102, MATH 110. The real numbers and completeness; sequences and series; limits, continuity, and uniform continuity; differentiation; the Riemann integral; sequences and series of functions and uniform convergence; metric spaces.

Texts: Abbott, *Understanding Analysis*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–7; Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, 3rd ed., ch. 1–7. Reference: [MIT 18.100](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-100a-real-analysis-fall-2020/).

### MATH 370 Mathematical Logic and Set Theory

Prerequisites: MATH 110. Propositional and first-order logic; soundness and completeness; compactness and models; undecidability and the incompleteness theorems; axiomatic set theory, ordinals, and cardinals.

Texts: Enderton, *A Mathematical Introduction to Logic*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–3; Enderton, *Elements of Set Theory*. Reference: [MIT 18.510](https://math.mit.edu/academics/undergrad/subjects/185x.html) (not on OpenCourseWare).

### MATH 221 Abstract Algebra I

Prerequisites: MATH 120, MATH 110. Groups, subgroups, and homomorphisms; cyclic, permutation, and quotient groups; group actions and the Sylow theorems; rings, ideals, and fields; polynomial rings.

Texts: Fraleigh, *A First Course in Abstract Algebra*, 7th ed., Parts I–IV; Artin, *Algebra*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–7. Reference: [MIT 18.701](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-701-algebra-i-fall-2010/).

### CS 340 Theory of Computation

Prerequisites: MATH 110. Finite automata and regular languages; context-free grammars; Turing machines; decidability and reducibility; time complexity, P, NP, and NP-completeness.

Texts: Sipser, *Introduction to the Theory of Computation*, 3rd ed., ch. 0–5, 7; Hopcroft, Motwani, and Ullman, *Introduction to Automata Theory, Languages, and Computation*. Reference: [MIT 18.404J](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-404j-theory-of-computation-fall-2020/).

### MATH 230 Complex Variables

Prerequisites: MATH 102, MATH 130. Complex numbers and analytic functions; the Cauchy–Riemann equations; contour integration and Cauchy's theorem; Taylor and Laurent series; residues and their use in evaluating integrals; conformal mappings.

Texts: Saff and Snider, *Fundamentals of Complex Analysis*, 3rd ed., ch. 1–6; Stein and Shakarchi, *Complex Analysis*, ch. 1–3. Reference: [MIT 18.04](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/).

### CS 350 Cryptography

Prerequisites: MATH 221. Classical ciphers; discrete logarithms and Diffie–Hellman; RSA and factorization; elliptic curve cryptography; digital signatures; the security notions behind them.

Texts: Hoffstein, Pipher, and Silverman, *An Introduction to Mathematical Cryptography*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–4, 6, 7; Paar and Pelzl, *Understanding Cryptography*. Reference: [Brown Math 158](https://npflueger.people.amherst.edu/math158-2015).

### MATH 260 Topology

Prerequisites: MATH 210. Topological spaces, bases, and continuous maps; product, subspace, and quotient topologies; connectedness and compactness; countability and separation axioms; an introduction to the fundamental group.

Texts: Munkres, *Topology*, 2nd ed., ch. 1–4, 9; Mendelson, *Introduction to Topology*, 3rd ed. Reference: [MIT 18.901](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-901-introduction-to-topology-fall-2004/).

## Instructional Model

Each course is studied from its primary text, with the reference course as the guide to pace and emphasis. There are no instructors; the student plans, studies, and checks their own work.

- **Primary text.** The main source of the course. The listed chapters define its scope; the supplementary text offers a second explanation or more advanced treatment.
- **Reference course.** Lecture notes, videos, and problem sets from MIT OpenCourseWare or another university. Where official solutions exist, they are the best way to check one's own work.
- **Weekly workload.** 170 hours over a 4-month term is about 10 hours per week per course, including problem sets. The program's app shows how many hours the courses in progress require each week and records the hours actually studied.
- **Due dates.** Each course's problem sets fall at weeks 4, 8, 12, and 15 of its term and the cumulative review at week 16, counted from the day the course starts.
- **Pace.** Terms move with the student: finishing early brings the next term forward, and falling behind pushes it back.

## Assessment Policy

A course is complete when all its assignments are submitted fully solved; there are no grades. The standard is mastery: the student works, checks, and redoes each problem until the solution is correct and complete.

| Course type | Assignments | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regular course | Problem Sets 1–4 | 25 problems each, covering the material up to its due date |
|  | Cumulative Review | 10 problems across the whole course |
| Seminar | Presentation | A recorded talk on the chosen topic |
|  | Paper | An expository paper of about 10 pages, typeset in LaTeX, with a bibliography |

- **Sources of problems.** Problems come from the primary text, the reference course, or are written by the student. Problems generated with AI tools are allowed only after the student checks them against the text.
- **Submission.** Each assignment is a PDF of the statements and full solutions. The student stores it wherever they choose, makes the link public so that anyone with it can open the file, and pastes the link in the app, which keeps only the link.
- **Checking.** Where the reference course publishes official solutions, the student compares their work with them. Elsewhere, the student checks results by independent methods, special cases, or the text's answers.
- **Prerequisites.** Courses taken before their prerequisites are allowed, but the student is responsible for the gap.

## Academic Integrity

Because the credential rests on published work, that work must be the student's own and honestly described.

- **Own solutions.** Every solution is written by the student. Consulting texts, notes, and discussions is encouraged; copying a solution is not.
- **AI tools.** AI may be used to explain concepts, suggest problems, or check reasoning. It may not write the solutions submitted as the student's work. Problems produced with AI are checked against the text before use.
- **Attribution.** Problems taken from a book or course are labeled with their source; adapted problems say so. Seminar papers cite every source they use.
- **Copyright.** Published files contain original or adapted problem statements with references, never reproduced pages of a textbook.
- **Accuracy.** Dates and completion records are not altered after the fact. A course that was skipped or left incomplete is shown as such.

## Credential and Verification

The graduate issues their own diploma and publishes a verification page; the proof of the degree is the work itself, open to anyone.

- **Diploma.** Created, edited, and published by the graduate, outside the app; its wording and design are the graduate's choice. The verification page links to it. Stating that the degree is self-directed and not conferred by an accredited institution is recommended, to avoid any confusion with an official title.
- **Verification page.** A web page file that the app generates at any time from what the student recorded, and that the student publishes on a web host of their choice (GitHub Pages, for example); the app does not host or publish it. It shows the student's name and one link, the start and finish dates, links to the diploma and this handbook, and every planned course by term, with its code, hours, dates, and the files of its assignments (or presentation and paper, for seminars).
- **What a visitor can check.** That each course has its full set of work; that the work matches the course's scope in this handbook; and that the dates are consistent with the program's pace.
- **What it does not certify.** The page proves that the work exists and is public. It does not prove who did the work or that it was done without help; readers judge that, as they would any portfolio.
